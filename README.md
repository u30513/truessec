## 📌 Introduction

The complexity of contemporary ICT products and services places the citizen
at a structural disadvantage: the properties that determine whether a product
is trustworthy are not observable at the point of use. Certification and
labelling exist to address that asymmetry, substituting an assessable signal
for an assessment the user is not in a position to perform.

The remedy, however, has been applied without coordination. Labels, seals and
marks have proliferated to the point of competing with one another rather
than informing, each carrying its own scope, methodology and degree of rigour.
Where dozens coexist, a label ceases to discriminate: the citizen cannot
reasonably distinguish a substantive certification from one obtained for
presentational purposes, and the signal is diluted by the abundance intended
to strengthen it.

## 🧩 The approach

TRUESSEC.eu examined the circumstances, barriers and benefits associated with
security and privacy labels, and issued recommendations intended to encourage
their adoption. The object of study was the certification landscape itself
rather than any particular technology, and the work proceeded along three
strands.

| Strand | Object |
|---|---|
| **Stakeholder engagement** | An online platform on which industry, academia, government and civil society could deliberate, contribute evidence and respond to emerging findings |
| **Analytical studies** | Examination of existing trust-enhancing labels, of the barriers and incentives governing adoption by industry, and of the conditions under which consumers accept them |
| **Recommendations** | A certification methodology, a catalogue of criteria for labels and certifications, regulatory measures to promote adoption, and a strategic agenda |

Each question was approached from four perspectives: socio-cultural, legal
and ethical, technological, and business. The premise is that trustworthiness
does not reduce to a technical property. A label certifying technical security
alone leaves untouched the grounds on which citizens actually withhold trust,
namely the handling of personal data, fairness, transparency, the recourse
available when something fails, and whether rights established in European law
are respected in practice. These are legal, ethical and social questions as
much as technical ones, and a proposal that neglects any of them will not
earn trust, and may serve to obscure its absence.

Particular weight was given to fundamental rights, treated as a criterion of
trustworthiness rather than as a consideration external to it. The work was
carried out between 2017 and 2018 by a consortium of universities, industry
bodies and civil-society organisations drawn from several European countries.

## 🛠 The assessment tool

The linked application is the assessment instrument developed in the course of
this work and presented to the European Commission. It renders the labelling
criteria as a structured questionnaire, so that the proposal could be
evaluated against actual products and services rather than debated in the
abstract.
